Grooming basics

How often should you groom your dog? A coat-by-coat guide

Susan, Pawgo Mobile Grooming September 10, 2026 6 min read

There's no universal number. A Poodle mix and a Labrador can live in the same house and need completely different grooming rhythms — because the question was never "how often", it's "how often does this coat need it".

The short answer: 4 to 8 weeks for most dogs

If your dog has a coat that grows or sheds continuously, a professional groom every 4 to 8 weeks is the range that works for most pets. Within that window, the right number depends on three things: coat type, shedding level, and how your dog spends its days.

Haircut coats (the ones that grow forever and need clipping) sit at the frequent end. Non-shedding, curly coats are the most demanding. Heavy double coats need seasonal attention rather than styling. Smooth-coated dogs sit at the relaxed end of the scale.

By coat type

Curl coats & non-shedding coats

Poodles, Bichons, Doodles, Maltese and similar coats don't shed out — the hair keeps growing and traps loose hair against the skin. Without regular clipping, that growth tangles and then mats.

Aim for every 4–6 weeks for a full groom. If you stretch to 10 or 12 weeks, you're not saving money; you're usually buying a mat-removal appointment on top of the haircut.

Long, silky coats

Yorkies, Maltese, Shih Tzu, Spaniels and long-haired mixes need brushing at home between visits regardless of schedule. For professional grooming, every 6–8 weeks keeps the coat manageable and the ears, feet and sanitary areas tidy.

Double coats

Huskies, Goldens, Labs, Pomeranians, Shepherds and Bernese-type coats blow (shed out) heavily twice a year and produce a steady stream of undercoat the rest of the time. These dogs rarely need a haircut — they need the undercoat actually removed.

Aim for a deshedding appointment every 6–8 weeks, with extra sessions either side of the two annual coat blows. During a coat blow, weekly brushing at home makes the difference between "shedding" and "snowing".

Smooth & short coats

Boxers, Beagles, Bulldogs, Labs and similar coats are low-maintenance but not zero-maintenance: nails, ears, sanitary trim and bath still matter. For these dogs, every 8–12 weeks is usually plenty, plus a 15-minute nail trim visit in between if nails grow fast.

Schedule by lifestyle too

The same dog can need different rhythms in different lives. A city dog walked on pavement wears nails faster but stays cleaner. A dog that swims, hikes or rolls in everything gets bathed more often. A dog with a history of ear infections needs those ears checked more regularly than the calendar suggests.

Age matters as well — puppies are often introduced to grooming gently and seniors often cope better with shorter, more frequent sessions than marathon grooms. Share your dog's age, health and lifestyle when you book so the appointment can be matched to them.

Signs it's already time

  • Nails clicking on hard floors. That's overdue, not decorative.
  • Hair over the eyes or dragging on the floor. A haircut coat has outgrown its last trim.
  • Scrunching or matting behind the ears, armpits or groin. Friction zones mat first.
  • A constant drop of loose undercoat in every room, or tufts coming out when you pet them.
  • That wet-dog smell that shows up a few days after a bath — often trapped dead coat and oil underneath.

What "grooming" actually includes

Frequency is only useful if the appointment covers the right things. A full groom — bath, blow dry, haircut, nail trim and sanitary tidy — is the complete reset. A deshedding appointment does the same wash-and-dry work but targets the undercoat instead of cutting length.

The rhythm that works

Pick a coat-appropriate interval, book it, and treat it like a standing appointment. Coats are far easier to maintain than to catch up — which is why the cheapest groom on your calendar is always the one you didn't let slide.
